Draw it

Draw lines, rectangles, circles, curves, freehand strokes, and dimensions in mm, with snapping, undo, and redo. Type a dimension to resize a side.

Give each part a role: the solid part and its holes, where heat comes in and goes out, and the joints between solids. Each solid has its own material.

Put a heat source or a probe at a single point, such as a small chip: with the Line tool, tap the point twice.

Model a plate of set thickness, the section of a long part per unit depth, or the section of a round part (axisymmetric).

Analyze it

The solver works while you draw and edit. A quick result shows at once, and a finer one replaces it when you stop.

  • Temperature °C
  • Heat flux W/m²
  • Liquid fraction %
  • Concentration kg/m³
  • See isotherms, heat flux arrows, the melt front, and the temperature along each fluid stream.
  • Type h for a convection edge, or have it worked out from the fluid and how it moves: still or moving air, water, or oil.
  • Play a run over time back with the time slider, and read when a probe reaches a temperature.
  • Read probes at a point, along a line, or over an area, and the heat into the part at each edge.
  • Work in SI units (mm, W, °C) or US units (in, BTU/h, °F).

Four analyses

  • Steady The temperature, the heat flux, and the heat through each edge, with the heat balance.
  • Over time How fast a part warms up or cools down from its starting temperature.
  • Melting and freezing The melt front and the liquid fraction, as ice melts or a casting freezes.
  • Diffusion Moisture, salt, or a gas spreading through a solid.

Learn with the examples

Each example has notes on what to look for, the theory value where there is one, and what to try next. Every example runs without a subscription, in every analysis.

Pick from metals, plastics, building and insulation materials, soils and rocks, and materials that melt, such as aluminum 6061-T6, copper, brick, mineral wool, ice, and paraffin, each with its properties and their sources. Air, water, glycol mixtures, and engine oil serve convection and fluid streams, and a list of surfaces gives emissivities for radiation.

Compare with theory

Thirteen examples follow cases with a known answer from heat transfer textbooks. When the run is done, the Results tab shows the theory value beside the app’s own value, and whether they agree within the stated tolerance.

Change the part and see how far the answer moves: make a fin longer, a wall thicker, or the insulation on a pipe thicker.

Theory in the notes

  • Heat through a wall A straight-line temperature drop and one heat flux through the wall, from Fourier’s law.
  • Cooling fin The tip temperature and the fin’s efficiency, from the fin equation.
  • Insulated pipe The critical radius of insulation, k/h: below it, more insulation lets more heat out.
  • Corner of a wall The heat through the corner per unit depth, kΔT((ℓ₁ + ℓ₂)/t + 0.55873), where ℓ₁ and ℓ₂ are the walls’ inside lengths, t is their thickness, and 0.55873 is the corner’s own shape factor.
  • Cooling a small sphere The whole sphere cools at one temperature, within 1% of the starting temperature difference, while h·r/k is 0.03 or less.
  • A radiation shield One shield with the plates’ emissivity halves the radiation between wide plates.
  • Counterflow heat exchanger The effectiveness and the log-mean temperature difference, from the ε–NTU relations.
  • Ice melting The position of the melt front over time, from Neumann’s solution.

Educational use only

Sketch Heat is for education and learning only. Its results are estimates from a simplified two-dimensional model. The calculation is verified against published exact solutions and benchmark cases, such as a plane wall, a cooling fin, and a suddenly heated block, but it has not been validated against measurements or tested in real-world scenarios.
